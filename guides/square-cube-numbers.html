Number & place value

Square numbers, cube numbers and roots

Years 5–6 5 min read Practise this →

A square number is what you get when a whole number is multiplied by itself: 5 × 5 = 25, so 25 is a square number. A cube number uses three copies: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.

The names come from shapes. 25 counters make a 5-by-5 square; 64 cubes stack into a 4-by-4-by-4 cube. That picture is worth keeping, because it explains why these numbers appear whenever you work with area or volume.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Read the notation properly

    5² is said “five squared” and means 5 × 5. 4³ is “four cubed” and means 4 × 4 × 4. The small raised number counts how many copies are multiplied, and it is called the index or power.

  2. 2

    Learn the squares to 12²

    1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144. They sit on the diagonal of a times tables grid, so if the tables are secure these are already half-known.

  3. 3

    Learn the small cubes

    1, 8, 27, 64, 125 and 1,000 for 10³. Those six cover almost everything a primary question will ask.

  4. 4

    Square root reverses squaring

    √49 asks “what number multiplied by itself gives 49?” The answer is 7. If you know the squares, you know the roots — it is the same list read backwards.

  5. 5

    Spot the gaps between squares

    1, 4, 9, 16, 25… the differences are 3, 5, 7, 9 — consecutive odd numbers. A neat pattern, and a quick way to extend the list without multiplying.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Reading 3² as 3 × 2 = 6.

✓ The index counts copies of the base, it is not a multiplier. 3² = 3 × 3 = 9.

✕ Thinking 5³ means 5 × 3.

✓ It means three 5s multiplied: 5 × 5 × 5 = 125.

✕ Forgetting indices come before multiplication in BIDMAS.

✓ In 2 × 3² you square first: 2 × 9 = 18, not (2 × 3)² = 36.

✕ Assuming square numbers are rare, so 1 does not count.

✓ 1 × 1 = 1, so 1 is a square number — and a cube number too.

The full reference list

Squares to 15²: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225.

Cubes to 10³: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000.

Primary expects the squares to 12² and the cubes to 5³ at minimum. The rest are a bonus that pays off quickly at secondary.

Where squares and cubes turn up

Area is measured in square units because you multiply two lengths; volume uses cubic units because you multiply three. A 6 cm square has an area of 36 cm² and a 6 cm cube has a volume of 216 cm³.

They also appear in Pythagoras' theorem at secondary, in standard form, and in any formula involving scaling: double the sides of a square and the area goes up four times, not two.

Units matter

Writing cm² or m³ is not decoration — it is part of the answer and marks are lost without it. A useful check: area answers always carry a squared unit, volume answers a cubed one, and perimeter or length answers a plain one.

Questions people ask

Why is it called ‘squared’?
Because the number of counters needed to build a square with that side length is exactly the number multiplied by itself. 4 × 4 counters make a 4-by-4 square.
Can a square number be negative?
Not when you square a whole number — a negative times a negative is positive. So square numbers themselves are always 0 or positive.
What is the square root of 2?
About 1.414. It is irrational, meaning it cannot be written exactly as a fraction or terminating decimal. Primary questions stick to perfect squares.
Is 0 a square number?
Yes, 0 × 0 = 0. Most primary lists start at 1 because 0 adds nothing useful.
