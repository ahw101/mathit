Fractions

Finding a fraction of an amount

Years 3–6 5 min read Practise this →

“What is 3/5 of 45?” is the most common fraction question in primary maths, and it has a two-step method that always works: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.

45 ÷ 5 = 9, then 9 × 3 = 27. Dividing first keeps the numbers small, which is why this order is better than multiplying first.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Divide by the denominator

    This tells you the size of one part. 45 ÷ 5 = 9, so one fifth of 45 is 9.

  2. 2

    Multiply by the numerator

    This counts how many parts you want. 9 × 3 = 27, so three fifths of 45 is 27.

  3. 3

    Check it is sensible

    3/5 is a bit more than half, so the answer should be a bit more than 22.5. 27 fits.

  4. 4

    For the reverse, divide by the numerator first

    If 3/5 of a number is 27, then one fifth is 27 ÷ 3 = 9, and the whole is 9 × 5 = 45. Same two steps, opposite order.

  5. 5

    Use the bar model when it is confusing

    Draw a bar, split it into denominator-many equal boxes, write the total above. Shade numerator-many boxes. The picture answers both directions.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Multiplying by the denominator and dividing by the numerator.

✓ Divide by the bottom, multiply by the top. Say it as a rhyme every time until it sticks.

✕ Answering with the discount instead of the price.

✓ Re-read the question. ‘Reduced by 1/4’ asks what you pay — the remaining 3/4.

✕ Giving a decimal when the context needs a whole number.

✓ 2/3 of 10 children is 6.67 — which means the question is wrong, or you have misread it. Quantities of people must come out whole.

✕ Dropping the units.

✓ £30 is not 30. In money and measures questions the unit is part of the answer.

Why divide first

3/5 of 45 can be done as (3 × 45) ÷ 5 = 135 ÷ 5 = 27, or as (45 ÷ 5) × 3 = 9 × 3 = 27. Identical answers.

Dividing first keeps the numbers small and usually turns the question into one you can do mentally. Multiplying first produces 135, which most children then have to write down. Same method, half the effort.

Reverse problems

“2/7 of a number is 18” is a question type that appears in every SATs reasoning paper, and it catches children who have only drilled the forward direction.

The logic is identical, reversed: if two sevenths is 18, one seventh is 9, and seven sevenths is 63. A bar model with seven boxes, two of them labelled 18 between them, makes it obvious.

The link to percentages

Percentages are just fractions with a denominator of 100, so the same method applies. 25% of 80 is 1/4 of 80 is 20. 60% of 45 is 3/5 of 45 is 27.

Questions people ask

What is the rule for finding a fraction of an amount?
Divide by the denominator, then multiply by the numerator. ‘Divide by the bottom, times by the top.’
How do I find the whole when I know a fraction of it?
Reverse the steps: divide by the numerator to find one part, then multiply by the denominator.
Does it matter whether I divide or multiply first?
Not to the answer. Dividing first keeps the numbers smaller and is usually easier mentally.
How is this different from multiplying fractions?
It is the same operation — ‘of’ means multiply. This method is just the efficient way to do it when one of the numbers is a whole.
