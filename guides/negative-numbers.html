Negative numbers: counting below zero

Years 4–6 6 min read Practise this →

Negative numbers are the numbers below zero: −1, −2, −3 and so on. Children meet them first as temperatures, then as floors below ground in a lift, then as overdrawn bank balances.

The arithmetic itself is not hard. What trips people up is that the minus sign does two different jobs — it marks a negative number, and it means “subtract”. Separating those two uses is most of the battle.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Picture the number line

    … −5, −4, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3 … Negative numbers run to the left of zero. Everything that follows is just movement along this line.

  2. 2

    Bigger looks smaller

    −7 is less than −2, even though 7 is bigger than 2. Further left means smaller. On a thermometer, −7°C is colder.

  3. 3

    Adding moves right, subtracting moves left

    Start at the first number and step. −3 + 5: start at −3, move 5 right, land on 2. 4 − 9: start at 4, move 9 left, land on −5.

  4. 4

    Difference means the gap

    The difference between −4 and 3 is the distance along the line: 4 steps up to zero, then 3 more. That is 7. Add the two sizes when the numbers are on opposite sides of zero.

  5. 5

    Two minuses together make a plus

    5 − (−3) means “start at 5 and take away a debt of 3”, which leaves you better off: 5 + 3 = 8. Subtracting a negative always moves you right.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Saying −7 is bigger than −2 because 7 is bigger than 2.

✓ Compare positions, not sizes. Further left is always smaller, so −7 < −2.

✕ Counting zero as a step when crossing it.

✓ From −2 to 3 is 5 steps, not 6. Zero is a position you pass through, not a step.

✕ Treating 5 − (−3) as 5 − 3.

✓ Subtracting a negative adds. 5 − (−3) = 8. If you can, rewrite it with the double sign removed before calculating.

✕ Assuming the answer to a subtraction must be positive.

✓ 3 − 10 is perfectly valid and equals −7. Nothing says the bigger number has to go first.

Contexts that make it obvious

Abstract number lines work for some children; contexts work for the rest. The three that carry the most weight:

  • Temperature. “It was −4°C overnight and rose 11 degrees by noon.” Natural, and the rise/fall language maps directly onto right/left.
  • Lifts and floors. Level −2 is two floors below ground. Going up three floors from −2 lands on 1.
  • Money. A balance of −£30 means you owe £30. Pay in £50 and you have £20.

What primary children are and are not expected to do

The National Curriculum asks Year 4 to count backwards through zero, Year 5 to interpret negative numbers in context and count forwards and backwards with positive and negative whole numbers including through zero, and Year 6 to use negative numbers in context and calculate intervals across zero.

Multiplying and dividing negatives — the “two negatives make a positive” rule for −3 × −4 — is secondary work. Do not rush it; a child who is secure with ordering and intervals is exactly where they should be.

A reliable drawing

When a child is stuck, have them draw the line and mark zero in the middle before doing anything else. Nine times out of ten the error was a direction error, and the drawing makes direction impossible to get wrong. It is slow, but speed comes later and only after the method is right.

Questions people ask

Is zero positive or negative?
Neither. Zero is the boundary between them. It is an even number, but it has no sign.
How do I write negative numbers properly?
With a short dash immediately before the digits and no space: −5, not − 5. Some books raise it slightly (⁻5) to distinguish it from subtraction, but that is not required.
When do children multiply negative numbers?
Normally Year 7 or 8. Primary stops at ordering, counting through zero and finding intervals.
What is the difference between −5 and 5?
10. They sit the same distance either side of zero, so the gap is 5 + 5.
