Dividing fractions: keep, change, flip

Year 6 6 min read Practise this →

Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal — the fraction turned upside down. Schools teach this as keep, change, flip: keep the first fraction, change ÷ into ×, flip the second.

It is a reliable rule, and it is worth spending five minutes on why it is true, because the “why” is what stops children flipping the wrong fraction.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Keep the first fraction exactly as it is

    Do not touch it. Only the second fraction changes.

  2. 2

    Change the division sign to multiplication

    ÷ becomes ×.

  3. 3

    Flip the second fraction

    Swap its numerator and denominator. 2/5 becomes 5/2. This is its reciprocal.

  4. 4

    Multiply as normal

    Tops across, bottoms across, then simplify.

  5. 5

    Whole numbers get a denominator of 1

    Dividing by 4 means dividing by 4/1, which flips to 1/4. So ÷ 4 is the same as × 1/4 — which is just halving twice.

  6. 6

    Convert mixed numbers first

    2½ ÷ 1¼ must become 5/2 ÷ 5/4 before anything else happens.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Flipping the first fraction instead of the second.

✓ Only the divisor — the one after the ÷ sign — is flipped. Say ‘keep, change, flip’ in that order, touching each part as you say it.

✕ Flipping both fractions.

✓ That gives the reciprocal of the answer. One flip only.

✕ Changing the sign but forgetting to flip, or flipping but leaving ÷.

✓ They are a pair. Write the new calculation out in full before computing anything.

✕ Expecting the answer to be smaller.

✓ Dividing by a fraction less than 1 makes the answer bigger. 3 ÷ 1/4 = 12, and that is correct — there are twelve quarters in three.

Why flipping works

Division asks “how many of these fit into that?”. 3 ÷ 1/4 asks how many quarters fit into 3. Each whole contains 4 quarters, so three wholes contain 12 — which is exactly 3 × 4.

More formally: dividing by 1/4 and multiplying by 4 have the same effect because 1/4 and 4 are reciprocals, and multiplying by a number then by its reciprocal gets you back where you started. The flip is not a trick; it is the definition of division restated.

Dividing by a whole number

2/3 ÷ 4 can be done two ways. Keep-change-flip gives 2/3 × 1/4 = 2/12 = 1/6. Alternatively, just multiply the denominator by 4: 2/(3×4) = 2/12. Same answer, and the second is quicker once children trust it.

Picture it: two thirds shared between four people. Each third splits into four, so the whole is now in twelfths, and each person gets two of them.

Checking with multiplication

If 3/4 ÷ 2/5 = 15/8, then 15/8 × 2/5 should return 3/4. Multiply it out: 30/40 = 3/4. It does.

That check is cheap and it is the only thing that reliably catches a wrong-fraction flip, because the arithmetic of a wrongly flipped division still looks tidy.

Questions people ask

What is a reciprocal?
The fraction turned upside down. The reciprocal of 3/4 is 4/3; the reciprocal of 5 is 1/5. A number multiplied by its reciprocal always gives 1.
Why does dividing by a fraction make the answer bigger?
Because you are asking how many small pieces fit into the amount, and small pieces fit many times. 3 ÷ 1/4 = 12.
Can I divide fractions without flipping?
Yes — with a common denominator you can divide the numerators: 3/4 ÷ 2/4 = 3 ÷ 2 = 3/2. It works but is rarely taught in UK schools.
When do children learn to divide fractions?
Year 6 covers dividing a proper fraction by a whole number. Dividing a fraction by a fraction is normally secondary, though it appears in extension work.
