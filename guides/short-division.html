Short division (the bus stop method)

Years 4–6 5 min read Practise this →

Short division — the “bus stop” — is the method for dividing by a single digit. You work left to right, one digit at a time, carrying whatever is left over into the next digit.

It is quicker than long division and children use it far more often. It also underpins converting fractions to decimals and simplifying, so it is worth getting fluent.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Draw the bus stop

    The number being divided goes inside, the divisor outside on the left, and the answer is built on the roof above — one digit directly above each digit inside.

  2. 2

    Divide the first digit

    How many times does the divisor go into it? Write that above. Anything left over is carried as a small digit in front of the next number inside.

  3. 3

    Carry the remainder and move right

    The carried remainder joins the next digit to make a new two-digit number. Divide that, write the answer above, carry again.

  4. 4

    Handle a first digit that is too small

    If the divisor does not fit, write 0 above that digit and carry the whole digit forward. 128 ÷ 4 starts with 4 into 1, which is 0 remainder 1, then 4 into 12.

  5. 5

    Decide what to do with the final remainder

    Three options: leave it as “r 2”, write it as a fraction over the divisor, or add a decimal point and zeros and keep going. The question will usually tell you.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ Writing the carried remainder as a separate answer digit.

✓ The remainder goes inside the bus stop, as a small digit in front of the next number — never on the roof.

✕ Skipping the leading zero when the divisor doesn't fit the first digit.

✓ 128 ÷ 4 must start 0, then 3, then 2 — and the leading zero is then dropped from the written answer. Omitting it in the working shifts every digit.

✕ Lining the answer digits up anywhere other than directly above.

✓ One answer digit sits above each digit inside. If they drift, the place value of the answer is wrong.

✕ Stopping at ‘r 3’ when the question asked for a decimal.

✓ Read the question. ‘Give your answer to 2 decimal places’ means adding a point and zeros and continuing.

Short or long?

Short division is for single-digit divisors — anything from 2 to 9, and 11 or 12 if the child is confident. Two-digit divisors generally need long division, where the subtraction is written out in full because it is too much to hold in your head.

Some children cope with 12 or 15 in a bus stop. There is no rule against it; the test is simply whether they can do the mental subtraction reliably.

Remainders in context

Word problems decide what the remainder means, and the maths alone cannot tell you. “47 children, 6 per minibus” gives 7 remainder 5, but you need 8 minibuses. “47 cakes shared between 6” gives 7 each with 5 left over, and the answer is 7. “£47 split 6 ways” gives £7.83.

Always re-read the question after dividing. Rounding the wrong way on a remainder question is one of the most common SATs reasoning errors.

Fractions to decimals

Any fraction is a division: 3/8 means 3 ÷ 8. Short division turns it into 0.375. This is how children convert fractions that are not in the standard list, and it is why fluency here pays off in the fractions topics later.

Questions people ask

Why is it called the bus stop method?
Because the layout looks like a bus shelter — a vertical line and a roof over the number being divided. It has no mathematical significance.
Can I use short division for two-digit divisors?
You can if you can do the mental arithmetic, but most children are more accurate with long division once the divisor is above 12.
How do I write the remainder as a fraction?
Put the remainder over the divisor. 947 ÷ 4 = 236 r 3 becomes 236 and three quarters.
What if the division never ends?
Some do not — 1 ÷ 3 = 0.333… recurring. Round to the number of decimal places the question asks for, or leave it as a fraction.
