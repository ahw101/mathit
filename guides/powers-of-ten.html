Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000

Years 4–6 5 min read Practise this →

Multiplying by 10 makes every digit ten times bigger, so each one shifts one column to the left. Dividing by 10 does the reverse. 100 shifts two columns, 1,000 shifts three.

Children are often taught “add a zero”, which works for whole numbers and then collapses the moment decimals appear — 3.6 × 10 is 36, not 3.60. Teaching the shift from the start avoids a reset in Year 5.

The method, step by step

  1. 1

    Count the zeros to get the shift

    × 10 shifts one place, × 100 two places, × 1,000 three places.

  2. 2

    Multiplying shifts digits left

    Each digit becomes worth ten times more. 3.6 × 10: the 3 moves from ones to tens, the 6 from tenths to ones. Result 36.

  3. 3

    Dividing shifts digits right

    Each digit becomes worth ten times less. 450 ÷ 100: the 4 moves from hundreds to ones, the 5 from tens to tenths. Result 4.5.

  4. 4

    Fill empty columns with zeros

    If shifting leaves a gap between the digits and the decimal point, a zero holds the place. 7 × 100 = 700; 7 ÷ 100 = 0.07.

  5. 5

    Keep the decimal point still

    Picture the digits sliding past a fixed point rather than the point hopping along the digits. Both give the same answer, but the sliding image survives contact with 0.07.

Worked examples

Where it usually goes wrong

✕ ‘Adding a zero’ to multiply a decimal by 10.

✓ 3.6 × 10 is 36, not 3.60. Shift the digits one column left instead — it works for every number.

✕ Shifting the wrong way when dividing.

✓ Dividing makes numbers smaller, so digits move right. Check the answer is smaller than you started with.

✕ Forgetting the placeholder zeros.

✓ 7 ÷ 100 = 0.07, not 0.7. Every empty column between the digits and the point needs a zero.

✕ Counting the digits in 1,000 instead of the zeros.

✓ 1,000 has three zeros, so three places. The leading 1 is not a shift.

Why the metric system is easy

Every metric conversion is a power-of-ten shift, which is why no multiplication is ever really needed:

  • km → m: × 1,000
  • m → cm: × 100
  • cm → mm: × 10
  • kg → g: × 1,000
  • litres → ml: × 1,000

Going the other way divides. A child who is fluent with place-value shifts can do every metric conversion on the KS2 curriculum without looking anything up — see metric units.

Chaining shifts

Multiplying by 20 is × 2 then × 10. Multiplying by 300 is × 3 then × 100. Dividing by 50 is ÷ 100 then × 2, or ÷ 5 then ÷ 10.

This is how mental multiplication of larger numbers actually works, and it is far faster than reaching for a column method. 14 × 300 = 14 × 3 = 42, then shift twice = 4,200.

Checking with estimation

Place-value shift errors produce answers that are out by a factor of ten, which is exactly the kind of error estimation catches. If 2.4 km ‘converts’ to 240 m, a moment's thought says that is wrong — a kilometre is roughly a thousand paces, so 2.4 km must be thousands of metres.

Questions people ask

Does the decimal point move, or do the digits?
Mathematically it does not matter, but teaching that the digits move keeps place value front and centre and avoids the ‘add a zero’ habit.
Why can't I just add a zero?
It only works for whole numbers. For decimals it is wrong — 3.6 × 10 is 36, and ‘3.60’ is still 3.6.
How do I multiply by 10,000?
Four zeros, so shift four places left. The pattern continues indefinitely.
What happens when you divide by 10 repeatedly?
The number keeps getting smaller and the digits keep sliding right: 5, 0.5, 0.05, 0.005. It never reaches zero.
